About

Clinical instinct, technical toolkit.

I'm Judah Akinlaja, a data scientist and ML engineer working where clinical knowledge meets applied analytics. My work is about one thing: solving healthcare data problems so better decisions get made.

My path into data science started in clinical science. That foundation matters: I don't just run models on health data, I understand what the variables represent, why patterns emerge, and what clinicians need from the numbers.

I studied at the University of Ilorin, Nigeria, building a deep understanding of human physiology and anatomy, disease pathways and the scientific method. I then moved to the University of Manchester for an MSc in Health Data Science, training in machine learning, statistical modelling, medical image analysis and clinical bioinformatics. Manchester gave me the technical toolkit. Nigeria gave me the clinical instinct.

Since then I've put both to work in very different settings: a teaching hospital's pathology department, a neuroscience research programme, a research laboratory, a private company, and the UK health system, including NHS England. The tools change from place to place. The job stays the same: get the data into a state people can trust, then answer the question that matters.

What drives me is a specific frustration: the gap between what health data could do and what it actually does. Hospitals sit on years of operational data that never gets analysed. I build the analytical bridges that close that gap, not with flashy AI, but with rigorous, interpretable work that people trust and use.

In the works

Why I'm building Health Care Data Solutions

During my time with NHS England, I kept running into the same thing. The people making decisions about care were rarely short of data. They were short of data they could trust, in a form they could use, at the moment they needed it. In one analysis, 41% of A&E attendances had no diagnosis code at all. No model fixes that. Someone has to do the careful work underneath first.

That gap is why I'm building Health Care Data Solutions, the healthcare consultancy arm of Helionr Limited, a UK-based IT firm. Helionr brings the engineering backbone, and I bring the clinical grounding and the health data experience.

We help health organisations with the whole journey, not just the last step:

  • Cloud architecture and administration: secure, cost-aware platforms for health data
  • Data engineering: pipelines and data quality, so the numbers can be trusted
  • Data analytics: reporting that answers the questions managers actually ask
  • Data science and statistical analysis: models with honest uncertainty
  • ML engineering: packaging, deployment and monitoring, so models keep working after the pilot

We're starting in the UK, because it's the health system I know from the inside and because the need is in front of us. In time, we want to take what we learn here to health systems around the world that face the same problems with fewer resources.

It's early days, and we're building carefully. If your organisation has a health data problem that keeps getting pushed to next quarter, I'd like to hear about it. Leave a note.

How I think

Four principles behind every project.

01

Start with the decision, not the dataset

The first question is never “what does the data say?” It's “what decision needs to be made, and what would change someone's mind?” Every project begins with the stakeholder's problem. The model is just the vehicle.

02

A model nobody trusts is a model nobody uses

Interpretability is not optional. It's the difference between a dashboard that gathers dust and an insight that changes how a ward operates. If the clinical team can't explain why the model flagged a patient, the work isn't finished.

03

Understand the money, not just the maths

Who commissions the work, who funds the intervention, and who carries the risk if it fails. Ignoring procurement, budgets and political context produces technically correct analysis that never reaches a decision.

04

Build for systems that don't exist yet

The long game: bringing rigorous health data science to markets where the infrastructure is still being built. The UK first, then health systems around the world. Sustainable, locally owned analytical capacity that compounds over decades.
